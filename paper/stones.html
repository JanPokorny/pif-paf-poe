The stones

Six types. Three of them move things, one refuses to be moved, and two dictate where the opponent may play. You each hold five, place one a turn, and three in a row wins — so a stone is never only a square, it is a square and an effect.

Shift

Name a direction. The row or column this stone sits in slides one step that way, and whatever falls off the end wraps around to the other end.

The middle row is full, so all three shuffle along and the one on the end comes back at the beginning. Only that row moves: the rest of the board is none of a Shift’s business.

2048

Name a direction. Every stone on the board slides that way as far as it can go, just as in the tile game.

Every stone on the board, not just the ones near it, and each of them as far as it will go — until it runs out of board or into another stone. The stone that was just played travels too.

Rotate

Pick one of the 2x2 blocks this stone belongs to. Those four squares turn one step clockwise.

The top-left block, one step round: each of the four stones takes the square clockwise of it, the Rotate itself included. A stone in the middle belongs to all four blocks and may pick any of them.

Mountain

Does nothing when placed, and nothing ever moves it. It blocks rather than forbids: other effects still happen, they just cannot move it or pass through it.

Somebody else’s 2048, naming down. Everything falls to the bottom and the Mountain stays where it was put — and nothing slides past it either, so a line it stands in packs up on both sides of it.

Magnet

Your opponent must place their stones adjacent to this one.

The rings are every square O may play on next: adjacent means sharing an edge, never a corner. A Magnet binds the opponent and never its owner, and it holds until another restriction replaces it — but if no free square would satisfy it, it does not apply that turn.

Stinky

Your opponent must place their stones not adjacent to this one.

The same thing the other way about: O is pushed off the four squares beside it, and the corners are all that is left. Only one restriction is ever in force — the most recently placed, whoever owns it — so playing either of these replaces the other.

What they are for

A line can be made by moving a stone into it rather than by placing one there, and a line you were about to complete can be taken apart by the stone your opponent plays next. Stones are never removed, so the ninth placement fills the board — and if nobody is in a line by then, the player who moved second wins.
